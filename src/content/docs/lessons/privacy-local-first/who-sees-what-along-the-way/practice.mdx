## Self-check

Answer in your head (or on paper) before opening the collapsible.

**1. What does "see" mean in this lesson, and what does it not mean?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

"See" means *technically able to observe the message contents or its metadata*. It does not mean *reads every message as a routine*. The CDN technically sees your traffic. Whether it logs the contents is a separate question. This is the path-versus-policy distinction from the previous lesson: the path is what is structurally possible; the policy is what the party at that step commits to doing with the ability.

</details>

**2. Name three parties involved at step 3 of the path (the CDN).**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The CDN itself; the CDN engineering team at the vendor; the vendor's site reliability engineers (SREs); and a third-party CDN service if the vendor uses one rather than running their own. Any three of these is a correct answer. The key fact for all of them: the CDN ends the encrypted connection, so the contents pass through as plaintext at this step. What gets logged is a vendor policy choice on top of the architectural fact.

</details>

**3. What is the platform's "safety review process," and why is it the most common path by which a person at the vendor reads a specific conversation?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The safety review process is the vendor's mechanism for catching messages that violate the vendor's usage policy. It typically combines an automated classifier (which flags conversations matching a pattern) with a human reviewer who reads the flagged content. It is the most common path by which a specific person reads a specific conversation because vendors generally do not have people read every conversation, but they do have people read flagged ones. The flag itself is a policy choice. The specifics vary by vendor.

</details>

**4. Why does the lesson cite Apple's Private Cloud Compute, and what does the citation establish?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The lesson cites PCC as a worked example of an architecture explicitly designed so that the vendor's own staff cannot see user data, including during active processing. The point is not to recommend a product. It is to show that the architectural-no-see property is technically possible when a vendor designs for it. Because that alternative exists, the typical shape (where the model service can see plaintext) is a design choice, not a law of physics. Vendors that advertise privacy should be evaluated on what their architecture actually enforces, not just on what their policy promises.

</details>

**5. What is the difference between consumer access and API access, and why does it matter for the party list?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Consumer access is what most readers of this lesson use: a vendor's website, with a CDN front door, on the seven-step path the previous lesson walked. API access is the direct connection a vendor offers to developers and apps. When an app reaches an AI provider through the API, the consumer-facing CDN is usually out of the path; the request goes directly from the app's server to the vendor's API endpoint. The parties along the API path are different from those along the consumer path. This is why vendor policies sometimes list "consumer data" and "API data" as separate categories: they are governed by different agreements and run through different infrastructure.

</details>

**6. What are controller and processor, and which is the AI vendor in two scenarios: (a) you use the vendor's website on your own initiative, (b) your employer has set up an enterprise account on the vendor's platform and you use it for work?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A *controller* decides what data is collected and what it is used for. A *processor* handles data on behalf of the controller. In scenario (a), where you use the vendor's website on your own initiative, you are dealing with the vendor as the controller. In scenario (b), where your employer has provisioned you an enterprise account, the employer is the controller and the AI vendor is the processor handling data on the employer's instructions. In (b), the employer's privacy policy is the one that applies first; the vendor's policy describes how the vendor processes data on the employer's behalf.

</details>

## Applied exercise: map a scenario

This exercise takes about 10 minutes. Use pen and paper or a text editor.

Pick one concrete scenario from your own life or work where you might use an AI tool. Examples: drafting an email at work on your employer's machine; asking a chatbot for cooking ideas on your home laptop; using a customer-facing AI feature in an app on your phone. Use something specific.

For your chosen scenario, write a short list (a paragraph or a bulleted list) naming the parties at each of the seven steps. You will not be able to fill in every step with confidence (some parties depend on which vendor and which network you are using); name the ones you can name, and mark the ones you cannot with a question mark.

Worked example for the scenario *"drafting an email at work on the employer-provided laptop, using the employer's enterprise AI account"*:

<details>
<summary>Show worked answer</summary>

1. **Keys to chat box.** Me. The employer-installed operating system (whatever monitoring software the IT department configured; I should ask). The browser (Chrome, in this case). Any extensions I installed and any extensions IT installed.
2. **Browser onto the network.** The office wifi network, operated by the employer's IT department. The employer's ISP. The public internet's routers.
3. **The CDN.** The vendor's CDN. The vendor's CDN engineering team. The vendor's SREs.
4. **Inside the model service.** The model service software. The vendor's engineering team. The platform's safety review process. Vendor staff with debugging access.
5. **The model and its hardware.** The vendor's hardware (or a cloud provider they pay; I do not know which from the outside).
6. **The reply comes back.** Same parties as forward direction, in reverse.
7. **The reply appears.** Me. Browser extensions. Anyone passing my desk who can see my screen. Anyone who later opens my conversation history (the employer's IT may be able to, depending on the enterprise setup).

Plus the controller/processor layer: because this is an enterprise account, my employer is the controller and the AI vendor is the processor. My employer's privacy policy is the one that applies first.

Three concrete next steps based on this map: ask IT what monitoring software is on this machine; check whether the employer has shared a written policy on what they expect employees to do with the enterprise AI account; open the vendor's enterprise privacy documentation (which is different from their consumer privacy policy).

</details>

After you have written your version, open the worked example to compare. You will probably have steps where your party list is shorter than the worked example, and steps where it is longer (because you know more about your specific situation than the worked example knows about hers). Both are fine. The exercise is the practice of pointing at each step and naming a party, not getting every party right on the first pass.

A few notes on what to skip and what to keep:

- **Skip vendor-specific implementation guesses.** You do not know exactly which hardware your chosen vendor uses, or which third-party CDN, or which cloud infrastructure provider. Mark those with "the vendor's, whoever that is" and move on.
- **Keep the controller/processor layer.** Many readers will have at least one scenario where an employer or institution sits between them and the vendor. That layer matters and is easy to miss.
- **Do not pre-empt the threat work.** Naming a party is not the same as deciding the party is dangerous. The previous lesson said "treating no one sees this as a default" is a pitfall. Treating "any party who could see this is bad" is the opposite pitfall. Both are wrong. The threat-categorization work comes in the third part of the track.

<Flashcard question="What is a site reliability engineer (SRE), and why are SREs on the party list?">

SREs are the people at the vendor who keep production systems running. They are on the party list because during incidents (outages, abuse events, debugging sessions) they may need debug access to live traffic. That access is the architectural ability that makes one of the lesson's cited examples possible: "a perimeter load balancer that terminates TLS may end up logging thousands of user requests wholesale during a troubleshooting session."

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is BYOK (Bring Your Own Key), and what does it change?">

A variant of the API tier where an app that uses an AI provider behind the scenes lets you bring your own API key (an account-level credential from the AI provider). When you supply your own key, the app routes your usage through the AI provider under your account, not the app maker's. The AI provider sees your usage. The app maker may see less, depending on how they built the BYOK flow.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is a controller, and what is a processor?">

If you use an AI tool through its website on your own initiative, the vendor is the controller. If your employer has provisioned you an enterprise account, the employer is the controller and the AI vendor is the processor handling data on the employer's instructions.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Name three local-side parties whose access to your messages does not pass through the vendor's infrastructure at all.">

Browser extensions installed in your profile; screen-recording or screen-sharing software running on your machine; anyone sitting at your shoulder; anyone who later opens your conversation history (if the history is stored on your machine or in your browser). Any three of those is a correct answer. The seven-step path describes the vendor side. Your local environment is its own list of parties.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is the practical takeaway: how does naming the parties change Aisha's behavior?">

It does not change *what* she pastes. That is the seed paragraph's job (the second lesson) and the postcard test's job (the first lesson). It changes whether she has a mental picture of *who is on the other end of each hop* when she pastes. The closer she gets to fluency with the party list, the more readable any vendor's privacy policy becomes, because the policy is describing the parties she now knows by name. Path picture + party picture + the right test = informed paste decisions.

</Flashcard>
