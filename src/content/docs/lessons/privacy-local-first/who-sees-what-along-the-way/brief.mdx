## What this lesson covers

The previous lesson walked the seven-step path a single AI message takes from your keyboard to the model and back, all in about three seconds. This lesson walks the same path again, but it asks a sharper question at each step: who is on the other end, and what can they see?

Some of the parties are obvious once you name them, like the vendor's own engineers and the people who maintain its model service. Some are not, like your local network operator, the vendor's content delivery network, and browser extensions you forgot you installed. The point of naming them is not anxiety. It is so that the next time you read a vendor's privacy policy and see a phrase like "service providers and business partners" or "site reliability engineers," you can map the phrase to a specific spot on the path you already know.

The lesson also introduces two formal terms (controller and processor) that appear in the cited vendor's policy and in other vendor policies subject to European data-protection law, and two distinctions (consumer access versus API access; bring-your-own-key apps) that change which parties show up where.

## Who this is for

You, if you have read the first three lessons of this track and now want a more populated picture of who is involved when you use an AI tool. The lesson uses the same teacher-named-Aisha example as the previous lessons: she is on her free period on Friday afternoon, having pasted one short test prompt this morning, and now she is wondering who, exactly, was on the other end of that trip.

The technical content here is a step denser than the first three lessons. The lesson introduces each new term when you would naturally meet it, not all at once, so the difficulty does not jump on you. By the end you will know enough to read a major vendor's privacy policy without skipping the paragraphs that name internal teams and external partners.

## What you'll learn

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Name the parties along each of the seven steps. Your-machine side: you, your operating system, your browser, any browser extensions. Network side: your local network operator (school IT, home router, coffee shop wifi), your ISP, the public internet's routers. Vendor side: the vendor's CDN, the CDN engineering team, the vendor's site reliability engineers, the platform's safety review process, the model service, and the hardware operator.
- Distinguish the technical ability of a party to observe your messages (the path) from the vendor's commitment about what they actually do with that ability (the policy)
- Recognize that consumer-website access and developer-API access are two different paths with different party lists, and know why vendor policies sometimes list "consumer data" and "API data" as separate categories
- Define controller and processor (the European data-protection terms the cited vendor's policy uses, and that other vendors operating in Europe typically use too), and identify which one a vendor is acting as in a given situation (for example, when your employer has provisioned you an enterprise account)
- Identify the local-side parties (browser extensions, screen-sharing software, shared access on your machine, anyone who later opens your conversation history) whose access does not run through the vendor's infrastructure at all

These are things you can do, not just things to know. The goal is that the next vendor privacy policy you read reads more like a list of named parties and their commitments, less like a wall of legal text.

## Key terms introduced in this lesson

These terms are defined in the lesson body when you first encounter them. They are listed here as a quick reference for readers who want to look one up later.

- **"See" (in this lesson):** technically able to observe the message contents or its metadata. Not the same as routinely reading every message.
- **Site reliability engineers (SREs):** the people at the vendor who keep production systems running. During incidents, they may need debug access to live traffic.
- **Safety review process:** the vendor's mechanism (typically an automated classifier plus human review of flagged conversations) for catching messages that violate the vendor's usage policy.
- **Consumer access versus API access:** two tiers of access to an AI vendor. Consumer access (the vendor's website) involves a CDN front door. API access (a direct connection for developers and apps) takes a different path with different parties.
- **BYOK (Bring Your Own Key):** an arrangement where an app that uses an AI provider behind the scenes routes your usage through your own API account, not the app maker's. Changes who sees what.
- **Controller:** the party that decides what data is collected and what it is used for. Comes from European data-protection law.
- **Processor:** the party that handles data on behalf of the controller. When your employer has set up an enterprise AI account, the employer is the controller and the AI vendor is the processor.
