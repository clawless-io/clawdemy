Friday afternoon. Aisha is on her free period, classroom door closed, the AI tool still open in the same tab she pasted a short test prompt into this morning. The reply came back in three seconds, just like the lesson said it would. The seven-step picture in her head is still warm. Now she has a sharper question. *Who, specifically, was on the other end of that trip? Who saw what?*

This lesson walks the same path again, step by step, but instead of asking *what happens* at each hop it asks *who can see what* there. Some of the parties are obvious once you name them. Some are not. By the end you will be able to point at any step of the chain and say, in plain language, who is observable there and what they could observe.

## What "see" means in this lesson

In this lesson, "see" means *technically able to observe the message contents or its metadata*. It does not mean *reads every message as a routine*. The vendor's CDN technically sees your traffic. Whether it logs the contents is a separate question. The vendor's site reliability engineers have access to debug logs. Whether they routinely read your specific conversation is again a separate question.

This is the path-versus-policy distinction from lesson 2.1. The path is what is structurally possible at each hop. The policy is what the party at that hop has committed to doing with the ability. Both layers exist. Both have to be read. This lesson is mostly about the first layer. Phase 4 is where the policy layer gets a proper rubric.

## Step 1: Keys to chat box (you, and your machine's other software)

The cast of characters at this step is small.

- **You.** Obviously.
- **Your operating system.** Every keystroke passes through the OS. The OS does not typically log what you type; the typed text is delivered to whichever window has focus and the OS forgets about it. But if you have software running that watches keystrokes (an enterprise security tool installed by your employer, an accessibility tool, malware), that software is also on this short list.
- **Your browser.** The browser holds the chat box and the text inside it. Browser extensions installed in your profile can read page content on most pages; an aggressive extension can therefore read the chat box too. Most extensions are well-behaved; some are not.

For Aisha, the relevant practical worries at this step are her school laptop's installed software (her district's IT may have set things up she did not pick) and any browser extensions she installed for other purposes that happen to have read-page-content permission.

**What this means for you.** Two cheap checks before any high-stakes paste. First: do you know what is installed on this machine? If your employer gave you the machine, ask whether any security or monitoring tool watches keystrokes or screens. The answer is sometimes yes. The answer is also information you have a right to know. Second: open the browser's extensions list. For each one, ask whether you need it on the page you are about to use. Disable the ones you do not need on that domain. Accept the rest as part of the cast.

## Step 2: Browser onto the network (your network's operators)

Once the message leaves the browser, the path runs across networks owned by other parties. Each one is a party.

- **Your local network's operator.** At a school, that is the district IT department or whichever provider runs the school's wireless. At home, that is whoever set up your router. The local operator sees that traffic is flowing between your machine and the AI tool's domain, and how much is flowing. Modern AI tools use encryption (the padlock icon in the URL bar). The operator sees the connection metadata but not the contents.
- **Your internet service provider (ISP).** The ISP sees the same kind of metadata. They see that your address is talking to the AI tool's address, when, and how much. They do not see the message contents through the encryption.
- **The public internet.** This is not a party, exactly. It is the set of routers and switches the message hops through to get from your ISP to the AI company's network. These routers see the destination address and the size of the packets. They do not stop to read contents. They do not log message contents for individual users.

For Aisha, the practical question is whether the people who can see her traffic metadata are people whose interests are aligned with hers. The school IT department probably is. A coffee shop's wifi network she might use on a weekend, less obviously.

**What this means for you.** The cheap check here is *whose network is this?* If the answer is "my employer's" or "my school's," the network operator can see (as metadata, not contents) that you are using a particular AI tool, when, and how often. If that visibility matters to you (because of the nature of your work, or because you would prefer this not be a topic of conversation with your manager), the question is worth asking *before* you start using the tool routinely. Switching to a personal network for personal-use AI is a one-time decision, not an ongoing burden.

## Step 3: The vendor's CDN (the vendor's edge infrastructure)

The first party that is part of the vendor's organization.

The CDN is the vendor's Content Delivery Network, which lesson 2.1 introduced. It is a network of servers spread across many places around the world. When Aisha's request arrives, the CDN picks a server near her. It receives her encrypted connection. It checks that the request looks valid. Then it forwards the contents inward to the vendor's model service.

The CDN receives the encrypted connection. That means the CDN sees the message contents in plaintext. This is architectural, not a policy choice. What the CDN logs, how long it keeps logs, and who at the vendor can read those logs are all policy choices on top of that architectural fact.

Apple's published engineering writing about Private Cloud Compute describes the typical commercial-cloud risk this way: *"a perimeter load balancer that terminates TLS may end up logging thousands of user requests wholesale during a troubleshooting session."* That sentence describes the risk. The system Apple designed to remove it (PCC) is the worked example we return to at step 5. The architectural ability is there in the typical shape. During incidents it sometimes gets used in ways even the vendor does not predict.

The parties at this step include the CDN itself, plus three groups of people:

- **The CDN engineering team.** The people at the vendor who write and operate the CDN software.
- **The vendor's site reliability engineers (SREs).** During an incident, the SREs may need debug access to live traffic. That access is what makes the troubleshooting-session example above possible.
- **A third-party CDN service, if the vendor uses one.** Some vendors run their own CDN. Some use a third-party CDN service. In the second case, that service is also a party here.

**What this means for you.** Knowing the CDN exists changes how you read a vendor's privacy policy. Look for a section that names what the CDN logs (versus what it forwards), retention periods on those logs, and who internally can access them. A policy that says only "we do not train on your messages" without saying anything about edge logs is making a narrower commitment than the sentence suggests.

## Step 4: The model service (the people who run the model)

Beyond the CDN, the message reaches what the previous lesson called the *model service*, meaning the vendor software that takes the plaintext message, hands it to the model, watches the reply being generated, and sends the reply back out.

The parties here include:

- **The model service itself.** Software that processes every message. By definition, the software sees the plaintext.
- **The engineering team that maintains the model service.** They write and update the code that runs your message through the model.
- **The platform's safety review process.** The vendor whose policy this lesson cites describes this explicitly. The policy mentions *"materials flagged for safety, security, or policy review"* and notes that the vendor may *"re-identify the Inputs or Outputs to enforce our Usage Policy."* The mechanism is usually an automated classifier that flags conversations matching a pattern. A human reviewer at the vendor may then read the flagged content.

  This is the most common way that a specific person at the vendor reads a specific user's specific conversation. It does not happen for every conversation. It happens for flagged ones. The flag is itself a policy choice. Check your specific vendor's policy for the exact wording.
- **Vendor staff with debugging access.** Same SRE category as at the CDN. Just a different layer.

There is also a class of party beyond the vendor's own organization. One major AI vendor's privacy policy is explicit about this. They *"disclose categories of personal data... with service providers and business partners for a variety of business purposes."* The policy lists examples: data hosting, compliance work, research, auditing, and payment processing. These are external companies the vendor contracts with. The vendor commits to using them only for the named purposes. The point: even when you trust the vendor, you also implicitly trust the chain of external providers the vendor relies on. Their privacy postures matter too.

**What this means for you.** Before pasting anything you would not want a human reviewer to see, look for the section of your vendor's privacy policy that describes safety review and human access. The wording varies. Three things worth checking in whatever wording your vendor uses. First, when does a human reviewer get involved? Every conversation, flagged conversations, or a sample? Second, what happens after the human reads? Deletion, or longer retention? Third, can human review be disabled by user setting? Some vendors let you. Some do not. The honest answer to *can a person at this company read this conversation if they have a reason* is usually *yes*. The policy is what tells you the conditions.

## Step 5: The model and its host hardware (the model itself, and the hardware operator)

The model is not a person. It cannot intend anything. What is at this step is just the math the model runs to produce the reply, and the hardware that math runs on.

But there are still parties here. The hardware is owned and operated by either the vendor itself (if they run their own data center) or by a large cloud infrastructure provider the vendor pays. The model service is running on someone's servers. The hardware operator generally does not read user data as a routine. That would breach the contract under which the vendor pays them. But the operator has privileged access to the hardware in the way any cloud provider does. The architectural ability sits on the same shelf as the other ones this lesson has walked.

This is the step where the architectural alternative from lesson 2.1 makes the biggest visible difference. Apple's Private Cloud Compute is one such alternative. Apple's design goal for PCC: *"This data must never be available to anyone other than the user, not even to Apple staff, not even during active processing."* The system has no privileged interfaces that would let site reliability staff bypass those privacy guarantees, even during a severe incident. The point of citing PCC is not to recommend a product. The point is that the architectural-no-see property is technically possible when a vendor designs for it. The alternative is real. The typical shape, where the model service can see plaintext, is a design choice. It is not a law of physics.

**What this means for you.** When a vendor advertises that "your conversations stay private," ask what the underlying architecture actually enforces. The answer usually fits one of three shapes.

(a) Routine commercial-cloud architecture with policy commitments on top. The most common shape today for consumer AI chatbots.

(b) A privacy-focused architecture with stronger enforcement. Apple PCC is the worked example cited above. A small but growing set of architectures fits this shape, sometimes only for specific parts of a vendor's service.

(c) A desktop or local-first architecture where the vendor's server is not in the path at all. Clawless, the desktop app this site's sister project ships, is the lesson 2.1 worked example. Its own knowledge base puts it plainly: *"your data does not pass through any Clawless servers."* The message goes from your computer to the AI provider you chose.

The vendor's posture sits in one of those buckets. The policy language they use should match the bucket. A mismatch is worth noticing.

## Step 6 and 7: The reply comes back, then appears (same parties, reverse direction)

The reply travels the same chain in reverse: model service to CDN to public internet to local network to browser to chat box. The same parties along the way handle the reply that handled the message. The CDN logs request and response; the model service sees the reply as plaintext on the way out; the human safety review (if it fires) reads the conversation as a whole, not just the message.

The one new party introduced on the return trip is, in a sense, *you again*. The reply lands in the chat box and is now visible in your browser, which means it is visible to anyone who has access to your browser tab (browser extensions, screen-sharing on a call, anyone sitting at your shoulder, anyone who later opens your conversation history if that history is stored). The party who "sees" the reply on the local side is not just the model service; it is the local-side surface you have.

**What this means for you.** Treat the reply with the same handling rules as the message. If the reply contains something you would not want stored (a generated email mentioning a person by name, a code snippet the model echoed back with a sensitive value, a summary that pulls forward something you typed earlier), the same parties along the path saw it as it traveled. The asymmetry between sent and received content is just content, not handling.

## Two distinctions that change the party list

The party list is not the same for every reader. Two distinctions, both named in the lesson body so far but worth pulling out:

**Consumer access versus API access.** Most readers use AI tools through the vendor's website. That is the consumer tier. The seven-step path with a CDN front door is the consumer-tier shape. There is a second tier called the *API*. Lesson 2.1 defined it as the direct connection a vendor offers to developers and apps. When an app reaches an AI provider through the API, the consumer-facing CDN is usually out of the path. The request goes straight from the app's server to the vendor's API *endpoint*. An endpoint is the specific network address the vendor publishes for software-to-software requests. The parties along the API path are different from the consumer path. Most chatbot users will never see the API tier directly. People who build apps on AI providers will. The point of naming it here is simple. If you read a vendor policy that lists "consumer data" and "API data" as different categories, you now know why.

**Bring Your Own Key (BYOK).** A variant of the API tier worth knowing about. Some apps that use an AI provider behind the scenes let you bring your own API key. The API key is an account-level credential you got directly from the AI provider. When you supply your own key, the app routes your usage through the AI provider under *your* account, not under the app maker's account. The privacy implication: the AI provider sees your usage. The app maker may see less, depending on how they built the BYOK flow. This is mostly an enterprise concern. But it matters for some prosumer apps. Worth knowing the term.

## A piece of formal vocabulary you may encounter: controller and processor

The vendor whose policy this lesson cites uses two specific words: *controller* and *processor*. The words come from European data-protection law. Vendors that operate in Europe typically use the same two words because the law requires them. Both are worth a short definition. They answer the "who is responsible" question at most of the steps above.

- A *controller* decides what data is collected and what it is used for. If you use an AI tool through its website on your own initiative, you are dealing with the vendor as the controller of that data.
- A *processor* handles data on behalf of someone else who is the controller. Say your employer has set up an enterprise AI account and you use it for work. Your employer is the controller. The AI vendor is the processor. The vendor handles the data on the employer's instructions. The employer's privacy policy is the one that applies. The vendor's policy describes how the vendor processes data on the employer's behalf.

The vendor whose policy we have been citing puts it the same way. The company "processes personal data on behalf of commercial customers" using its commercial services. When your employer has provisioned you an enterprise account on the vendor's platform, the vendor says *"the commercial customer is the controller, and you can review their policies for more information about how they handle your personal data."*

Knowing the two words helps you read these policies. The next time you see *"we act as a processor in the following cases..."* you know the sentence is shifting responsibility to the controller. That controller may be your employer, your school, or another institution that sits between you and the vendor.

## Common pitfalls

**Treating "no one sees this" as a default.** The architectural reality is that some party sees your messages at almost every step of the path. The right question is *which party* and *what is their policy*, not *whether* the path is observable. If you read a vendor's policy assuming "no one reads it unless I do something wrong," you may be surprised. The policy may say human review fires on flagged conversations. The vendor's safety classifier may later flag something innocuous.

**Confusing "the vendor doesn't train on this" with "the vendor doesn't store this."** These are two different commitments. A vendor can honestly say it does not use your messages to improve its models. The same vendor can still store the messages in operational logs for a fixed retention window. Both commitments matter. Do not collapse them into a single mental model.

**Forgetting about the local side.** The chat box is on your screen, in your browser, on your machine. Browser extensions, screen-recording software, anyone sitting at your shoulder, anyone who later opens your conversation history. All of them have a path to that content. None of those paths pass through the vendor's infrastructure. The seven-step path describes the vendor side. Your local environment is its own list of parties.
