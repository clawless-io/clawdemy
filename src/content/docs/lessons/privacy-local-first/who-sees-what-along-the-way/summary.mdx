The previous lesson walked the path your message takes through an AI tool. This one walked the same path again. Same seven steps. Different question. At each step, who is on the other end, and what can they see?

The answer at every step turns out to be at least one party. Sometimes more than one. Sometimes the party is obvious. Sometimes it is not.

## "See" means *technically able to observe*

Throughout the lesson, *see* is shorthand for technically able to observe the message contents or its metadata. It does not mean reads every message as a routine. The CDN technically sees your traffic. Whether it logs the contents is a separate question. This is the path-versus-policy distinction from the previous lesson. The path is the architectural floor. The policy is what the party at that step commits to doing with the ability.

## Parties at each step

| Step | Parties on the other end | What they can see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Keys to chat box** | You, your operating system, your browser, any browser extensions | Whatever you type, in plaintext |
| **2. Browser onto the network** | Your local network operator, your ISP, the public internet's routers | Connection metadata (who is talking to whom, when, how much). Encryption hides the contents. |
| **3. The CDN (vendor's front door)** | The CDN itself, the CDN engineering team, the vendor's site reliability engineers, third-party CDN services (if used) | Message contents in plaintext (the CDN ends the encryption; what gets logged is a policy choice) |
| **4. Inside the model service** | The model service software, the engineering team that maintains it, the platform's safety review process, vendor staff with debugging access | Message contents in plaintext, plus the reply as it is generated |
| **5. The model and its hardware** | The hardware operator (vendor or cloud infrastructure provider) | Message contents in plaintext, with the caveat that routine access is rare and constrained by contract |
| **6. The reply comes back** | Same parties as the forward direction, in reverse | Reply contents (the asymmetry between sent and received is content, not handling) |
| **7. The reply appears** | You again, plus browser extensions, screen-sharing, anyone with shared access, anyone who later opens your conversation history | Whatever the reply contains |

## Controller and processor

The cited vendor's privacy policy uses two specific words from European data-protection law: *controller* (decides what data is collected and what it is for) and *processor* (handles data on behalf of the controller). Vendors that operate in Europe typically use the same terms because European data-protection law requires them. If you use an AI tool through its website on your own initiative, you are dealing with the vendor as the controller. If your employer has set up an enterprise account and you use it for work, the employer is the controller and the vendor is the processor. The employer's privacy policy is the one that applies first. The vendor's policy describes how the vendor processes data on the employer's behalf.

## Two access tiers and one variant

- **Consumer access** runs through the seven-step path with a CDN front door. Most readers of this lesson use AI tools this way.
- **API access** is the direct connection a vendor offers to developers and apps. The consumer-facing CDN is usually out of the path. Different parties along the way. The reason vendor policies sometimes list "consumer data" and "API data" as separate categories.
- **Bring Your Own Key (BYOK)** is an API-tier variant where an app uses an AI provider behind the scenes but routes your usage through *your* API account, not the app maker's. The AI provider sees your usage; the app maker may see less.

## The architectural alternative, in one paragraph

The architectural alternative the previous lesson named (where the user's computer talks directly to an AI provider, with no consumer-facing CDN or vendor middleware in between) is one shape that changes the party list significantly. Apple's Private Cloud Compute is another shape: cloud-based, but designed so that the vendor's own staff cannot see user data, including during active processing. The point is that the typical shape (where the model service sees plaintext) is a design choice, not a law of physics.

## Common pitfalls

Treating *no one sees this* as a default leads to surprise when the vendor's safety classifier flags something innocuous. Confusing *the vendor does not train on this* with *the vendor does not store this* collapses two commitments into one. Forgetting about the local side (browser extensions, screen-sharing, shared access) misses parties that the seven-step path never touches.
