## What this lesson is

Lesson 1.2 is the closing lesson of Track 6 (Privacy and Local-First AI), Phase 1 (Orientation). It is the personalization move: lesson 1.1 turned a privacy cloud into a list of three named worries; lesson 1.2 gives that list an address. By the end, the reader has written one paragraph in their own words naming what they are protecting and from whom. The paragraph is short. It is also the artifact everything else in the track stacks on.

The lesson body runs approximately 12 minutes. All five derivative artifacts (summary, practice, cheatsheet, references, this brief) are complete.

## Who this lesson is for

The primary persona is Sarah in the Aisha configuration, continuing directly from lesson 1.1. Aisha has read the previous lesson, named three worries (surveillance, storage and leak risk, vendor lock-in), and recognized that the worries are generic. They apply to anyone using any AI tool. They do not yet apply to her, in her job, with her students, in her district. This lesson is the move that personalizes them.

The lesson speaks directly to that shape. The Khan-warmth register carries forward from 1.1: curious, not paranoid; practical, not expert; looking for something she can write and act on today, not a lecture on threat-modeling methodology.

## What capability it delivers

By the end of this lesson, a reader in the Sarah position can:

- Write a seed paragraph, in their own words and their own situation, naming specific assets and the adversary categories that actually apply to them
- Identify which of the four asset buckets (information about people in their care, information about themselves, credentials and account access, original work in progress) contain their real assets
- Name the five adversary categories relevant to AI-tool use and select the ones that apply to their situation
- Apply the 3-step recovery when stuck: three buckets with one specific instance each for assets; the possession-cost filter for adversaries; the one-sentence prompt for the whole paragraph
- Avoid the three pitfalls (borrowed threat model, aspirational asset list, treating the paragraph as final) on the first pass

These are capabilities, not facts. The goal is not that Aisha can describe a threat model; it is that she writes one, in her own handwriting, today.

## How it closes Phase 1

Phase 1 (Orientation) has one job: replace undirected worry with a directed starting point. Lesson 1.1 does the cognitive-reframe move (cloud to list). Lesson 1.2 does the personalization move (list to your situation). Together they make Phase 1 complete.

The paragraph Aisha writes in lesson 1.2 is the seed the rest of the track grows. Phase 2 (data-flow trace) shows her what actually happens to her assets when she presses Enter. Phase 3 (threat models) gives her a four-category vocabulary for the threats her adversaries pose. Phase 4 (vendor rubric) operationalizes her asset list into a ten-minute repeatable check for any tool. Phase 5 (local-first architecture) shows how architectural choices can eliminate some adversaries by design. Lesson 6.6 returns to the paragraph and grows it into a full personal privacy plan.

The lesson names the seed-to-harvest arc explicitly so Aisha knows the paragraph has a path, not just a function.

## Calibration anchor

Voice is calibrated against lesson 1.1 (why-your-worry-is-rational), the most recently shipped Track 6 lesson. 1.1 established the Aisha continuation pattern, the no-helplessness guard structure (every risk section closes with something Aisha can do), and the Khan-warmth register. 1.2 continues Aisha's arc: she left 1.1 with a list; she enters 1.2 from the parking lot before first period, recognizing the list is generic. The lesson's tone is the same as 1.1: a person helping another person think clearly, not a security professional briefing a non-expert.

## Vendor-naming posture

The body follows Anonymization Path A. No vendor brand names appear in body-claim positions. Derivatives follow the same rule. References.mdx is the one exception: it cites EFF SSD sources by URL and title, which is attribution, not endorsement.

## Review cadence

Review every 6 months. The two-question framework and asset-and-adversary vocabulary are derived from EFF SSD "Your Security Plan," which EFF updates on its own cadence (typically annual or as-needed). The core concepts are durable; the specific EFF quotes should be verified against the current SSD text on each review. A major SSD restructure could shift the framing the lesson cites; the 6-month cadence covers typical drift but is not designed to catch a major restructure. If SSD changes its six-question vocabulary substantially, the lesson will need a full revision pass on the body.
