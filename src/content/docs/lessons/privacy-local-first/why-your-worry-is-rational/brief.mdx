## What this lesson is

Lesson 1.1 is the opening lesson of Track 6 (Privacy and Local-First AI), Phase 1 (Orientation). It is a motivational concept lesson: its job is to give the Sarah persona a list where she had a cloud. It does not teach data-flow mechanics, threat-modeling vocabulary, or vendor policy specifics; those come in Phases 2, 3, and 4. What it teaches is that privacy worry is rational, that it has a finite and nameable shape, and that Sarah has a concrete action she can take right now for each of the three concerns.

The lesson body runs approximately 12 minutes. All five derivative artifacts (summary, practice, cheatsheet, references, this brief) are complete.

## Who this lesson is for

The primary persona is Sarah in the Aisha configuration: a non-technical person who has been handed an AI tool by an institution (a school, an employer, a professional workflow) whose data-handling she did not choose. Aisha teaches eighth-grade English and has been told her school is adopting an AI tool for progress reports. She is not against the tool. She wants to know what happens to student names, grades, and parent phone numbers when she types them into a box on a screen she did not choose.

The lesson speaks directly to that shape. Aisha is the concrete case; "you" is whoever is reading. The persona is Khan-warmth: curious, not paranoid; practical, not expert; looking for a list she can act on, not a lecture on surveillance theory.

## What capability it delivers

By the end of this lesson, a reader in the Sarah position can:

- Name three privacy concerns (surveillance, storage and leak risk, vendor lock-in) in her own words, without jargon
- Apply the postcard test (would I write this on a postcard?) before pasting anything sensitive
- Apply the what-if-leaked-tomorrow test (if this conversation appeared in the news next year, what is the consequence?) as a storage-risk gut check
- Know that there are multiple layers of settings beyond the first toggle, and that the first one is rarely the whole picture
- Begin distinguishing tools that protect privacy architecturally from tools that protect it by promise

These are capabilities, not facts. The goal is not that Sarah knows what surveillance is; it is that she acts differently the next time she is about to paste something sensitive.

## How it advances Phase 1

Phase 1 (Orientation) has one job: replace undirected worry with a directed starting point. Lesson 1.1 does the cognitive-reframe move (cloud to list). Lesson 1.2 does the personalization move (list to your situation). Together they make Phase 1 complete.

This lesson does not resolve any of the three worries. Resolution is the work of the later phases: Phase 2 (data-flow trace) resolves Surveillance, Phase 3 (threat models) formalizes breach risk, Phase 4 (vendor rubric) operationalizes Vendor lock-in, Phase 5 (local-first architecture) addresses architectural vs. promise-based protection at a deeper level. The lesson names these forward references explicitly so the reader knows the worry has a path, not just a name.

## Source material

This lesson is original Clawdemy authorship drawing on:

- **EFF: "AI Chatbot Companies Should Protect Your Conversations From Bulk Surveillance"** (CC BY). Grounds the Surveillance section: the intimacy framing ("most sensitive information"), the chatbot-as-diary observation.
- **EFF: "Privacy First: A Better Way to Address Online Harms"** (CC BY). Grounds the architectural-vs-promise distinction in the Vendor lock-in section.
- **Mozilla Foundation: Privacy Not Included, AI chatbot privacy article.** Grounds the Storage section (100 percent security is impossible, human review), the Vendor lock-in section (18 documentation links, the verbatim researcher quote), and the Common pitfalls section (multi-layer settings, training opt-out specifics).
- **Mozilla Foundation blog: AI privacy data article.** Supplements the Vendor lock-in framing with additional researcher commentary.

The three-worry framework, postcard test, what-if-leaked-tomorrow test, and no-helplessness structure are original editorial work. No regulatory citations appear in this lesson (Phase 1 is motivational; GDPR, CCPA, and rights-request framing enter in Phase 3 and Phase 6).

## Calibration anchor

Voice is calibrated against brand.md Exemplar A (the Aisha opening paragraph), which this lesson opens with verbatim. Khan-warmth register. Not threat-alert; Aisha wants to understand, not be warned. The no-helplessness guard (Principle 5 of the Track 6 curriculum constitution) is enforced across the body: every worry section closes with a concrete action Sarah can take, not with an open threat.

## Vendor-naming posture

The body follows Anonymization Path A. All vendor-body-claims use "one major chatbot provider." The verbatim Mozilla researcher quote cites the tool by name as a matter of faithful quotation; that exception is the only brand name in the lesson body. Derivatives follow the same rule. References.mdx is the one exception: it cites sources by URL, which is attribution, not endorsement.

## Review cadence

Review every 3 months. The three-worry framework is durable and does not depend on any specific vendor policy. The specific details about settings (retention windows, training opt-out availability) will drift as vendor policies change. On each review, check whether the settings examples in the Common pitfalls section are still accurate for at least one major tool, and whether the "18 documentation links" observation remains a useful illustration of the Vendor lock-in complexity problem.
