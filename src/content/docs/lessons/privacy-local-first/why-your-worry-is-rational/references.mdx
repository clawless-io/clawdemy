## Going deeper

A short list of durable resources for readers who want more than the lesson covers.

- **[EFF: "AI Chatbot Companies Should Protect Your Conversations From Bulk Surveillance"](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/12/ai-chatbot-companies-should-protect-your-conversations-bulk-surveillance).** The EFF article that grounds the Surveillance section of this lesson. Goes further than the lesson does on what EFF asks of chatbot providers: keep less user data within reach of law enforcement, and make three promises to users (fight bulk orders for user data in court, give users advance notice before complying with a legal demand, and publish periodic transparency reports counting the legal demands they receive). The lesson borrows EFF's framing of intimacy ("most sensitive information"); the article gives the full context, including EFF's argument that chat logs deserve the same warrant protection as email.

- **[EFF: "Privacy First: A Better Way to Address Online Harms"](https://www.eff.org/wp/privacy-first-better-way-address-online-harms).** EFF's longer white paper arguing that many online harms are best addressed by doing privacy first: comprehensive privacy law built on data minimization, so companies collect and keep only what they strictly need. Useful background for the Vendor lock-in section. The lesson's architecture-versus-promise test is Clawdemy's own framing, not EFF's; the paper supports its premise in a line worth remembering: "The less that is collected, the less that can be used against us."

- **[Mozilla: "How to Protect Your Privacy From ChatGPT and Other AI Chatbots"](https://web.archive.org/web/20250408105319/https://foundation.mozilla.org/en/privacynotincluded/articles/how-to-protect-your-privacy-from-chatgpt-and-other-ai-chatbots/).** The Mozilla Privacy Not Included curated article that grounds the Storage and Vendor lock-in sections. Covers the multi-layer settings problem (account-level, app-level, browser-level, feature toggles) the lesson introduces in the Common pitfalls section. Mozilla moved Privacy Not Included on 11 August 2026 and continues the work as Product Reviews in its Nothing Personal magazine, so the original URL now serves a notice rather than the article. Mozilla directs readers to the Internet Archive for the old material, which is what this citation points at: the capture of 8 April 2025.

- **[Mozilla Foundation blog: "AI & User Privacy: Here's What You Need To Know"](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/ai-privacy-data-chatgpt/).** The Mozilla Foundation blog post that grounds the constant-surveillance framing in the Surveillance section and the "18 privacy documentation links" observation in the Vendor lock-in section. The eighteen-link figure is for ChatGPT; the post also covers Gemini and Copilot, and includes the verbatim researcher quote the lesson cites. Useful if you want to see the full researcher commentary this lesson draws from.

- **[EFF Surveillance Self-Defense: "Your Security Plan"](https://ssd.eff.org/module/your-security-plan).** EFF's plain-language guide to building a personal threat model. More general than AI tools specifically, but the underlying structure (who are you protecting information from, what information, what are the consequences) is exactly what lesson 1.2 of this track asks you to practice. Worth reading before or after the lesson 1.2 exercise.

## Source limitations

These sources are good. Naming what each one is weaker on helps you use them well.

**EFF: "AI Chatbot Companies Should Protect Your Conversations From Bulk Surveillance" (December 2025 article).** This article frames its demands of chatbot providers (fight bulk orders in court, give users advance notice of legal demands, publish transparency reports) in U.S. constitutional terms, around the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement. Readers outside the U.S. should treat the legal framing as illustrative; the underlying advice (keep less data, tell users about government requests) is jurisdiction-neutral. The article is also an advocacy piece, not a neutral survey: it foregrounds the worst-case bulk-surveillance scenario more than the typical-day usage pattern. Read it for what providers *should* do, not for a balanced picture of what most providers actually do today.

**EFF: "Privacy First: A Better Way to Address Online Harms" (white paper).** This is a longer-form argument for comprehensive privacy legislation as the first response to many online harms. Its scope is broader than AI specifically (it covers children's safety, news, competition, and more), and it argues for law rather than for particular product designs; the lesson draws only on its data-minimization premise. Readers expecting a focused AI-tool analysis will need to filter the AI-relevant parts from the broader policy argument. The paper is also U.S.-centric in its legal references.

**Mozilla Privacy Not Included: "How to Protect Your Privacy From ChatGPT and Other AI Chatbots" (curated article).** This article is a snapshot of the AI-tool privacy landscape at one point in time. Vendor policy updates happened faster than Mozilla's review cycles even while the program was running, and the article is now fixed at April 2025, so treat any specific settings-path instruction in it as historical. Treat its framework (the multi-layer settings problem, the documentation-complexity problem) as durable; treat its per-product specifics as a starting point for your own verification against the vendor's current documentation.

**Mozilla Foundation blog: "AI & User Privacy: Here's What You Need To Know".** This blog post grounds the documentation-complexity observation (the eighteen-document figure) and the researcher commentary the lesson quotes. The figure is a snapshot; documentation counts shift as vendors add, remove, and consolidate policy pages. The lesson cites the figure as a useful illustration of the complexity, not as a stable count. The blog post also leans advocacy in tone, like the broader Mozilla Privacy Not Included program; read it for the diagnosis of the complexity problem, not as a neutral comparison of vendors.

**EFF Surveillance Self-Defense: "Your Security Plan".** This is EFF SSD's introduction to personal threat modeling; it is the source lesson 1.2 builds on directly. For the purposes of this lesson 1.1 (which only forward-references it), the limitation worth naming is that SSD is one well-considered framework, not the only valid one. Other personal threat-modeling frameworks exist (enterprise standards, journalist-specific guides) and would name the same underlying concepts differently. SSD is also U.S.-centric in its worked examples even though the underlying concepts are jurisdiction-neutral.
