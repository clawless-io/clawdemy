A huge number of real questions have the same shape: out of some fixed number of tries, how many succeed? How many of 10 incoming emails are spam? How many of 100 visitors sign up? How many of a model's 5 predictions are correct? Each is a count of successes in a fixed number of yes-or-no trials, and the distribution that answers them all is the **binomial distribution**. It is the discrete workhorse, the counterpart to the previous lesson's continuous bell, and it shows up anywhere you count "how many out of N."

## When is it binomial? Four conditions

A situation is binomial when all four of these hold:

- **A fixed number of trials, n.** You decide up front how many tries: 10 emails, 5 predictions, 100 visitors.
- **Each trial has two outcomes.** Success or failure, yes or no, the single-trial case called a **Bernoulli** trial (spam or not, correct or wrong).
- **A constant probability of success, p.** Each trial has the same chance p of success.
- **Independent trials.** One trial's outcome does not change the others (the independence idea from the probability lessons).

The binomial random variable is then **X = the number of successes in the n trials**. If any condition fails (the probability drifts, or the trials influence each other), the simple binomial does not apply, the same fine print that ran through the probability lessons.

## The probability of exactly k successes

To find the chance of exactly **k** successes in **n** trials, you need two ingredients: how likely any one specific arrangement of k successes is, and how many such arrangements there are.

Any one specific sequence with k successes and (n - k) failures has probability `p` multiplied by itself k times, and `(1 - p)` multiplied by itself (n - k) times. But the k successes can fall in many different positions among the n trials, and each arrangement counts. The number of ways to choose which k of the n trials are the successes is written **C(n, k)** (read "n choose k"), a counting number. Putting it together:

```
P(exactly k successes) = C(n, k) x p^k x (1 - p)^(n - k)

  C(n, k)          how many ways k successes can be arranged among n trials
  p^k              the probability of the k successes
  (1 - p)^(n - k)  the probability of the (n - k) failures
```

Make it concrete with **three coin flips** and the question "exactly 2 heads" (so n = 3, p = 0.5, k = 2):

```
C(3, 2) = 3      (the 2 heads can land as HHT, HTH, or THH)
P(exactly 2 heads) = 3 x (0.5)^2 x (0.5)^1 = 3 x 0.125 = 0.375 = 3/8
```

Check it by listing all 8 equally likely outcomes (HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT): exactly three of them have two heads, so 3/8. The formula and the count agree.

Now a less symmetric case that looks like AI. A model is **80% accurate** (p = 0.8). Over **5** independent predictions, what is the chance **exactly 4** are correct?

```
C(5, 4) = 5      (the one wrong prediction could be any of the 5)
P(exactly 4 correct) = 5 x (0.8)^4 x (0.2)^1 = 5 x 0.4096 x 0.2 = 0.4096
```

About a **41%** chance of exactly 4 right out of 5. Notice the formula handles p = 0.8 just as easily as the coin's 0.5.

## The expected count: n times p

You do not need the full formula to answer "how many successes should I expect on average." The expected value of a binomial is simply

```
E[X] = n x p
```

Five predictions at 80% accuracy: expect 5 x 0.8 = **4** correct. A hundred visitors at a 3% sign-up rate: expect 100 x 0.03 = **3** sign-ups. This is the expected value from the previous lesson, specialized to counting, and it matches intuition exactly: n trials, each succeeding a fraction p of the time. (For completeness, the variance is n x p x (1 - p), largest when p is near 0.5.)

## Exactly versus at least

A common trap: "exactly k" and "at least k" are different questions. The formula gives **exactly** k. For "at least k," you add up the probabilities of k, k+1, all the way to n, or, when k is small, use the complement trick from the probability lessons. "At least one success" is almost always easiest as `1 - P(zero successes) = 1 - (1 - p)^n`. Reaching for the exact-k formula when the question says "at least" is a frequent and avoidable error.

## Why this matters when you use AI

The binomial is the natural model for counting successes, which AI does constantly.

- **Accuracy is a binomial count.** A model's number of correct predictions on a test set of n examples is a binomial count: n trials, each correct with some probability p, the model's true accuracy. This is why an accuracy measured on a small test set is noisy, and it is the foundation for asking how confident you can be in a reported accuracy (the inference phase, next).
- **Conversions and rates.** Sign-ups out of visitors, clicks out of impressions, defects out of items inspected, all are binomial counts, and the n-times-p expected value is the back-of-envelope estimate. A/B testing, in the next phase, compares two such binomial rates.
- **The bridge to the normal.** When n is large, the binomial's bumpy bar chart smooths into the bell of the previous lesson. That is a first glimpse of the next phase's central limit theorem and the reason the normal approximation is used for large counts.

When you read "the model got 47 of 50 right" or "3% of visitors converted," you are reading a binomial count, and this lesson is how to reason about the chances behind it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Using the binomial when the conditions fail.** If the success probability drifts between trials or the trials are not independent, the simple binomial does not apply. Check the four conditions first.
- **Confusing "exactly k" with "at least k."** The formula gives exactly k; "at least" requires a sum or a complement. Mixing them up is a common error.
- **Dropping the C(n, k) factor.** The probability of one specific arrangement is not the answer; you must multiply by the number of arrangements, or you will badly undercount.
- **Treating n times p as guaranteed.** The expected count is a long-run average, not a promise: 5 predictions at 80% gives an expected 4 correct, but any single run of 5 might yield 3 or 5.

## What you should remember

- The **binomial distribution** models the number of successes in **n** independent yes-or-no trials with a **constant** success probability **p**; all four conditions (fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independence) must hold.
- **P(exactly k) = C(n, k) x p^k x (1 - p)^(n - k)**: the chance of one arrangement times the number of arrangements. Three coins give P(2 heads) = 3/8; a 80%-accurate model gives P(4 of 5 correct) about 0.41.
- The expected number of successes is the shortcut **E[X] = n x p** (5 predictions at 80% gives 4 expected).
- **"Exactly k" is not "at least k"**; the latter needs a sum or the complement (`1 - P(none)` for "at least one").
- In AI the binomial underlies **accuracy as a count of correct predictions**, **conversion and click rates**, and, for large n, the **normal approximation** that leads into the next phase.
