## A note on how these cases are cited

This is the copyright lesson, so accuracy cuts both ways. Every legal claim below is placed in one of three buckets, and each carries the same limits stated in the lesson. A decided ruling is a fact of record from a single trial court (for one case, now also an appeals court), cited with its limits (trial-court level or, for one ruling, appeals-court level; fact-specific; appealable or already on appeal), never downgraded to merely "contested." A settlement is reported as a fact, with no inference about who was right. A genuinely open question is marked "as of November 2026, unresolved." This corner of the law moves fast, so a non-author reviewer re-verifies every case, date, and status live before publish and on each freshness sweep; this lesson is first in every sweep. None of this is legal advice.

### Decided rulings, with limits

- **Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence** (U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware, partial summary judgment February 11, 2025; U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, No. 25-2153, decided September 29, 2026). The court held that a startup's copying of a legal-research company's material, used to build a competing search tool, was not fair use. Limits on record: the court expressly limited its reasoning to a non-generative AI tool, and the decision turned on its own facts. Current status: on September 29, 2026 the Third Circuit (Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves and Bove; opinion by Judge Montgomery-Reeves) affirmed, holding in its words that "Thomson Reuters's materials are copyrightable and that ROSS's use was not fair." Authors Alliance describes it as "the first federal appellate ruling on whether copying works to train an AI system is fair use"; the opinion itself does not say so. The court described the tool as "not a generative AI" and, in footnote 7, discussing a pending generative AI case in New York, wrote that "The concerns raised in that separate case do not apply here." Sources: the [Third Circuit opinion, No. 25-2153, text on court](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/252153p.pdf); the opinion issued by the District of Delaware (docket 1:20-cv-00613); Authors Alliance analysis of [the district court ruling](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2025/02/13/thomson-reuters-v-ross-the-first-ai-fair-use-ruling-fails-to-persuade/) and of [the Third Circuit affirmance](https://www.authorsalliance.org/2026/10/02/third-circuit-affirms-in-thomson-reuters-v-ross-the-court-gets-merger-wrong-and-does-a-disservice-to-fair-use/). The lesson keeps this to "a legal-research company" and "a startup" for readability; the material at issue was Westlaw editorial headnotes.
- **Bartz v. Anthropic, the ruling** (U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, summary judgment June 2025). The court held that training an AI on books the company had lawfully bought was fair use and highly transformative, while downloading and keeping pirated copies of books was not fair use and was infringement. Limit on record: the fair-use-on-training holding applied to the named plaintiffs. Source: [Authors Guild coverage](https://authorsguild.org/news/mixed-decision-in-anthropic-ai-case/). The lesson pairs this with the Kadrey ruling to show that trial courts have not agreed.
- **Kadrey v. Meta Platforms** (U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, June 25, 2025). The court granted summary judgment for the AI company on the training question, but the judge stressed that he was ruling narrowly, that the plaintiffs had argued their case poorly, and that in most future cases he expected such training to be found infringing, not fair. The fair-use ruling is not on appeal as of November 2026: the judge denied leave for an interlocutory appeal of the downloading part of the ruling on July 8, 2026, and the separate claim about distributing books while torrenting is still in the district court, with a summary-judgment hearing set for February 2027, so appeal would follow a final judgment. Sources: [Norton Rose Fulbright analysis](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/); for the scheduling order and the February 25, 2027 hearing date, [ChatGPT Is Eating the World, March 27, 2026](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/03/27/kadrey-v-meta-gets-scheduling-order-summary-judgment-motion-hearing-for-distribution-claim-wont-be-heard-until-feb-25-2027/) (a case tracker; check the docket for later changes); for the July 8, 2026 denial, [ChatGPT Is Eating the World, July 8, 2026](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/07/08/judge-chhabria-denies-kadrey-book-authors-motion-to-seek-an-interlocutory-appeal-of-downloading-part-of-ruling-in-fair-use-decision/).
- **Thaler v. Perlmutter** (U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, affirmed March 18, 2025; U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case on March 2, 2026). The courts held that, under the Copyright Act, an author must be a human, so an image its creator said a machine had made entirely on its own could not be registered. Limit on record: the case concerned a work claimed to have no human involvement at all, and it did not decide how much human input an AI-assisted work needs. Sources: the [D.C. Circuit slip opinion in No. 23-5233](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf) (PDF, 24 pages); [Skadden client alert](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/03/appellate-court-affirms-human-authorship). This is the same "creativity machine" case the course discusses, updated to its 2026 posture.

### A settlement, reported with no merits inference

- **Bartz v. Anthropic, the settlement.** After the ruling above, Anthropic reached a deal to settle the piracy part of the case, agreeing to pay at least 1.5 billion dollars and to destroy its pirated copies. A class settlement like this needs a judge's approval, and the court (Judge Martínez-Olguín, N.D. Cal.) granted final approval and entered final judgment on July 20, 2026; the court noted the release covers claims about past acquisition and copying through August 25, 2025. Some non-class members and one objector have filed notices of appeal, mainly about fees, so payments may be delayed. The release covers past conduct only, not model outputs or future conduct. Sources: [Authors Guild, final approval report](https://authorsguild.org/news/court-grants-final-approval-anthropic-copyright-settlement/); [Writer Beware, appeals update](https://writerbeware.blog/2026/07/23/anthropic-settlement-update-final-settlement-approved/); [Copyright Alliance summary](https://copyrightalliance.org/). Discipline that must survive: a settlement is a deal to end a case, not a court finding, and settling is not an admission of anything. No derivative hardens the figure past "at least 1.5 billion dollars," and none infers wrongdoing from the settlement or implies that payments have been made, which could not be confirmed as of November 2026.

### Genuinely open questions

- **The New York Times v. Microsoft and OpenAI** (U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York). The Times says the companies trained on millions of its articles without permission; the companies agree they used the articles and argue fair use. A judge let much of the case move forward in 2025 (a motion to dismiss was partly denied in April 2025), but there has been no ruling on the core question, no trial date, and no settlement as of November 2026. Update: in this case (now part of MDL No. 3143 before Judge Stein, S.D.N.Y.) the parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment on fair use on September 4, 2026, and no ruling has issued. This was the course's 2024 anchor example and it stays at status level in the lesson. Sources: [case overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_v._Microsoft_and_OpenAI); [AI Lawsuit Tracker](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/new-york-times-v-openai/).
- **Allen v. Perlmutter** (U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado). This is the AI-assisted line, exactly where the fight now sits. An artist made a prize-winning image with an AI tool after hundreds of prompts and revisions, was refused registration, and is challenging that refusal in court. Cross-motions (No. 1:24-cv-02665) were fully briefed by early 2026, and there is no ruling as of November 2026. Sources: [court docket, Allen v. Perlmutter, No. 1:24-cv-02665 (D. Colo.), on CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69198079/allen-v-perlmutter/) (the copyright office's cross-motion was filed January 16, 2026 and the artist's reply on February 27, 2026); [background on the work, Théâtre D'opéra Spatial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9%C3%A2tre_D%27op%C3%A9ra_Spatial). The lesson omits the artist's name and the work's title for a cleaner read.
- **Andersen v. Stability AI** and **Getty Images v. Stability AI (United States)** (both U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California). Other suits, brought by visual artists and by a large image library, are working through the same California court. Both are in active pretrial with no merits ruling on copyright or fair use as of November 2026; the Getty motion to dismiss was decided on April 23, 2026 (the DMCA claim was dismissed with leave to amend; the other claims proceed), and in Andersen, summary judgment and trial are set for 2027; check the docket. Source: [AI Lawsuit Tracker, Getty v. Stability](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/getty-v-stability-ai/).
- **The general training question.** Whether it is fair use to train AI on copyrighted work is, as a general matter, genuinely open. The individual rulings above are decided facts; the general question is open because those decisions are narrow, fact-specific, and conflicting. Updated October 31, 2026: one of them, Thomson Reuters v. Ross, has now been reviewed, and the Third Circuit affirmed. That ruling concerns a tool that was not a generative AI, so it narrows the open question rather than closing it. The lesson never states, in either direction, that training is or is not fair use as a settled rule.

### A non-US decision, quarantined

- **Getty Images (US) Inc v Stability AI Ltd** [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), UK High Court, judgment November 4, 2025. A related case in the United Kingdom reached a decision in late 2025 that mostly went against the image library. This ruling sits under a different country's law, does not bind United States courts, and is itself being appealed (the High Court granted Getty permission to appeal in December 2025; the Court of Appeal reportedly heard the appeal on November 11 and 12, 2026, and had not issued a judgment as of mid-November 2026). Sources: [IPKat on the grant of permission to appeal, January 2026](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2026/01/permission-to-appeal-granted-in-getty.html); [Pinsent Masons analysis](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law). The lesson carries this as one clearly labeled non-US line only.

### Agency guidance, not binding law

- **US Copyright Office, "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability"** (published January 29, 2025). Framed throughout the lesson as the Office's own registration practice, not a final word from the courts. Its position: typing prompts alone does not provide enough human control to make the user the author, because the same prompt can produce many different outputs; but a human's creative selection, arrangement, and modification of AI output, and human-authored expression that shows up in the result, can be protected. Worked example: the Office registered the comic book Zarya of the Dawn as a compilation for the human selection and arrangement of AI images plus the human-written words, while the individual AI-generated images on their own were not protected. Sources: [US Copyright Office AI initiative](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/), and the [Part 2 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf). Limitations: this is agency guidance; the courts remain the arbiter.

## The course's own worked examples

- **Authors Guild v. Google (Google Books)** is the transformative-use precedent the course draws on when explaining the purpose factor: a court found that scanning books to make them searchable added something genuinely new. The lesson keeps the factor generic in the body and names the precedent here.
- **The monkey-selfie case** (Naruto v. Slater) is the course's older worked example of the human-authorship rule: a photograph taken by an animal could not be copyrighted because there was no human author. The lesson trims it from the body for length; it makes the same point as the Thaler case.
- **Heart on My Sleeve**, the 2023 AI-made track that mimicked two famous musicians and was pulled from streaming services, is the course's example for the copying-versus-style question. It is kept in the lesson as a dated 2023 historical marker.
