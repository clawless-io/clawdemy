## What you'll learn

This is the fourth lesson in our adaptation of Stanford CME 295 Lecture 1 (Transformer). The full course materials are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/). The first three lessons in this lecture covered tokens, embeddings, and the single-head attention mechanism. This lesson is what is actually inside a real transformer layer: not one attention head, but many of them running in parallel, each looking at the same sentence through a different lens. We'll build the intuition for why one head is not enough, walk through the split-run-concatenate pattern that makes multi-head attention work, and end with what real model specifications mean when they say "12 heads."

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the attention lesson (earlier in this lecture) is strongly recommended. We build directly on the query, key, and value vectors from that lesson. If those terms feel unfamiliar, read the attention lesson first; this lesson assumes the single-head mechanism is already familiar. The embeddings lesson is useful context for what flows into attention, but not strictly required.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Explain why a single attention head is structurally limited at tracking multiple kinds of context at once
- Describe the split-run-concatenate pattern that turns one big projection into h smaller parallel attention computations
- Walk through the dimension flow in a multi-head computation, from input embedding to per-head outputs to the final concatenated result
- Recognize multi-head configurations in real model specifications (e.g., "12 heads at 64 dim each, total 768")

## Time and difficulty

- **Read time:** about 22 minutes
- **Practice time:** about 15 minutes (dimension arithmetic on paper, plus a quick inspection of a real model's head count)
- **Difficulty:** standard
