## What you'll learn

This is the fourth lesson in our adaptation of Stanford CME 295 Lecture 2 (Transformer-based models & tricks). The first three lessons covered the parts of the original 2017 transformer that have changed since: position embeddings, normalization, and attention efficiency. This lesson opens a new arc on what kinds of transformer-based architectures the field has built. We start with **encoder-decoder transformers** (the original 2017 architecture is one) and walk through the **T5 family** (T5, mT5, byT5) and its distinctive pretraining objective: **span corruption** instead of next-token prediction. We also cover the lecturer's framing of why the field eventually moved on from encoder-decoder toward decoder-only architectures (which most modern LLMs are). The lecturer is brief on T5; we stay within that brevity. Course materials are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/).

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the transformer block lesson (in our Lecture 1 adaptation) is required reading. We assume you understand what an encoder is, what a decoder is, what cross-attention does, and what next-token prediction looks like. The text generation lesson (in our Lecture 3 adaptation) is helpful background for the contrast with span corruption.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Identify the encoder-decoder architecture from the original 2017 transformer and explain what each half does
- Name the three members of the T5 family (T5, mT5, byT5) and what makes each distinct
- Walk through the span corruption pretraining objective and how it differs from next-token prediction
- Explain the lecturer's framing of why the field eventually moved to decoder-only architectures despite T5's strengths

## Time and difficulty

- **Read time:** about 18 minutes
- **Practice time:** about 12 minutes (a span-corruption walk-through on a small example, plus a comparison of T5's training objective with next-token prediction)
- **Difficulty:** standard
