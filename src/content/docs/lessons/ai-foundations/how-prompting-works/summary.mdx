**A prompt is the conditioning data for the model's next-token loop.** Lesson 7 made the model want to follow instructions. Prompting is the lever you actually pull. Three patterns account for almost everything you write at a prompt: zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought. The art is choosing the conditioning that makes the desired response a likely continuation of the input you supplied.

This summary is the scan-it-in-five-minutes version. The full lesson walks through each pattern with worked examples, names where each one earns its place and where it gets in the way, and closes on the structural reason any instruction-tuned model is vulnerable to prompt injection.

## Core ideas

- **Three patterns dominate.** Zero-shot (just ask), few-shot (show examples then ask), chain-of-thought (think step by step before answering). Almost every prompt you write is one of these or a combination.
- **Zero-shot is the default.** Works when the task is one the SFT training data covered well (translation, summarization, classification with common labels, simple Q&A, format conversion) and when the output format is not picky. Cheapest pattern, easiest to write, leverages the instruction-tuning bias directly.
- **Few-shot earns its place when format or labels are not standard.** A few examples teach the model the shape you want and it continues the pattern. The underlying phenomenon is **in-context learning**: the model is doing pattern-completion on the fly, not changing weights.
- **Few-shot has diminishing returns fast.** Most of the benefit shows up by the third or fourth example for typical tasks. Twenty rarely beats five well-chosen ones. Examples should span the variants you care about; format matters more than wording.
- **Chain-of-thought helps on multi-step reasoning.** "Let's think step by step" or showing input-reasoning-output triples nudges the model to produce intermediate tokens before the final answer. Each intermediate token conditions the next; the model gets more total compute on the problem.
- **Chain-of-thought hurts on tasks that do not need it.** Simple lookup, single-step classification, summarization of straightforward text: forced CoT adds tokens, can introduce confident-sounding intermediate errors, and rarely improves accuracy. Reach for CoT when the task actually has a chain to walk.
- **System and role prompts shape conversations but are not enforcement.** *"You are a helpful tutor"* is guidance the model is biased toward following, not a sandbox. The model treats it as input that conditions the next-token distribution, not as law.
- **Prompt injection is structural.** Instruction-tuned models cannot fully distinguish operator instructions from instructions hidden in user-supplied data. At the token level, both are just text that conditions the next-token loop.
- **Mitigations reduce the gap; they do not close it.** Instruction-hierarchy training, channel separation between system and user prompts, output filtering, and sandboxing the surface area an injected instruction can reach all help. None of them turn the bar into a wall, because the underlying mechanism (instruction-following over input tokens) is what makes the model useful.
- **Pitfall: chasing magic words.** Each pattern has a mechanism. The structural choice (zero vs few vs CoT) swamps the wording choice. Stop hunting incantations.
- **Pitfall: forced CoT.** If the task is not multi-step reasoning, CoT often costs you tokens without improving accuracy.
- **Pitfall: trusting a confident reasoning chain.** A CoT chain that sounds reasoned can still arrive at a wrong answer; the steps may be plausible-sounding rationalizations of an early commitment.
- **Pitfall: prompting versus fine-tuning.** Prompting is per-call and free of training cost. Fine-tuning persists across calls without paying token cost. Different tools, different cost profiles.

## What changes for you

Before this lesson, the difference between a prompt that works and one that does not was likely a matter of intuition or trial-and-error. After it, you have a frame: name the pattern, name the task type, predict whether the pattern fits the task. When a chat assistant produces something useless, the most productive first move is to ask whether the prompt actually specified the task in enough detail; the model is a context-conditioned prediction loop and vague conditioning produces vague output.

When you read about prompt-engineering "tricks" or copy-pasted prompt templates, you can locate them in one of the three patterns and ask what mechanism they are leveraging. When you build any application that puts a model on top of user-supplied or web-fetched text, you can recognize the prompt-injection surface area and design around it rather than designing as if the system prompt is a guarantee.

**Zero-shot asks the model.**\
**Few-shot shows the model.**\
**Chain-of-thought walks the model through it.**
