## Self-check

A short retrieval pass. Answer each question in your head (or on paper) before opening the collapsible. Active retrieval is where the learning sticks; rereading is comfortable but does much less.

**1. What is a prompt, mechanically?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

A prompt is the input tokens you control. The model is doing what the earlier lessons described: take a sequence of tokens, predict the next token, append it, repeat. The prompt is just the prefix that conditions that loop. The instruction-tuning covered in our Lecture 5 lesson adds a *bias* on top so that instruction-shaped input produces response-shaped continuations rather than continuations of similar text. The art of prompting is choosing the conditioning that makes the desired response a likely continuation.

</details>

**2. Name the three dominant prompting patterns and describe each in one sentence.**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Zero-shot:** just ask, no examples. Leverages the model's instruction-tuning bias directly. **Few-shot:** show a small number of input-output examples before the actual input; the model continues the pattern. **Chain-of-thought (CoT):** ask the model to produce intermediate reasoning tokens before its final answer (either by adding *"let's think step by step"* or by demonstrating the reasoning shape in few-shot examples).

</details>

**3. Why does few-shot prompting work, and what is the underlying phenomenon called?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The model is doing pattern-completion: it sees a structured sequence of `(input, output)` pairs and infers, from the examples alone, what shape the next output should take. No weights change at inference time; the "learning" happens entirely in the forward pass, conditioned on the examples in the context window. The phenomenon is called **in-context learning**.

</details>

**4. Where does few-shot stop scaling, and what should you do when you hit those limits?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Three places. (a) **Diminishing returns:** most of the benefit shows up by the third or fourth example for typical tasks; the marginal example does less and less. (b) **Context window:** examples eat tokens, so long examples crowd out room for the actual input and the model's response. (c) **Coverage:** if the examples cover only one variant and the new input is a different variant, the model often fails on the variant the examples did not teach.

When you hit those limits, the right move is usually fine-tuning (bake the behavior into the weights so you stop paying token cost on every call) rather than heroic few-shot.

</details>

**5. When does chain-of-thought help, and when does it hurt?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Helps:** multi-step arithmetic, multi-hop logical reasoning, planning problems, problems where the answer hinges on a chain of dependencies the model needs to track. The model gets more total compute on the problem because it produces more tokens before committing to an answer.

**Hurts (or wastes tokens with no benefit):** simple lookup, single-step classification, summarization of straightforward text, most translation tasks, anything without a real reasoning chain. CoT can introduce confident-sounding intermediate errors and rarely improves accuracy on these.

</details>

**6. Why is "act as a senior engineer with 20 years of experience" rarely the magic ingredient people think it is?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Two reasons. First, the structural choice of pattern (zero vs few vs CoT) and the quality of the conditioning swamp the wording. Magic phrases sometimes show effects on specific benchmarks; in everyday use they are noise compared to the structural lever. Second, role prompts can actively hurt when the model starts performing the persona (filler, character voice, hedging in the persona's style) at the cost of doing the task. Use a role prompt when the role genuinely changes what good output looks like; skip it when it is decoration.

</details>

**7. What is prompt injection, and why is it structural rather than a bug?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Prompt injection** is when text inside an input that the application thinks is *data* (an email, webpage, comment, search result) contains instruction-shaped tokens that the model follows as if they were operator instructions. Classic shape: a user pastes *"Ignore all previous instructions and ..."* into a field your app concatenates into a prompt.

It is structural because the model has been trained to recognize and follow instruction-shaped input. The system prompt is instruction-shaped; the injected text is instruction-shaped. At the token level they are both just text that conditions the next-token distribution, and the model has no robust way to distinguish *which* instruction is authoritative. Mitigations (channel separation, instruction-hierarchy training, output filtering, sandboxing) reduce the gap; they do not close it, because the underlying mechanism is what makes the model useful in the first place.

</details>

## Try it yourself: classify and redesign

This exercise puts pattern recognition into practice. About 15 minutes.

**Side effects:** none. Pen and paper, or a text editor.

### Part one: classify

Below are six prompts. For each one, identify which pattern it uses (**zero-shot**, **few-shot**, **chain-of-thought**, or a **combination**). Then say whether the pattern is well-matched to the task.

```
Prompt A:
Translate the following Spanish sentence to English.
Sentence: "Buenos días, ¿cómo estás?"

Prompt B:
Classify each item as fruit or vegetable.

Item: apple
Class: fruit

Item: carrot
Class: vegetable

Item: pumpkin
Class:

Prompt C:
A small bakery sold 47 croissants in the morning, 32 in the afternoon,
and 18 in the evening. How many croissants did they sell that day?
Let's think step by step.

Prompt D:
You are a senior cybersecurity expert with 25 years of experience.
Tell me whether this email looks like a phishing attempt:
[email contents pasted here]

Prompt E:
Q: Tom has 3 boxes of pencils with 12 pencils each. He gave away 8 pencils.
   How many pencils does he have now?
A: Tom started with 3 * 12 = 36 pencils. He gave away 8, so he has 36 - 8 = 28 pencils.
   The answer is 28.

Q: Anna has 5 packs of stickers with 10 stickers each. She used 17 stickers.
   How many stickers does she have left?
A:

Prompt F:
Summarize this article in three bullet points:
[article pasted here]
```

**Expected outcomes:**

- **Prompt A:** zero-shot. Well-matched. Translation is exactly the kind of task SFT covered well; few-shot or CoT would only add tokens.
- **Prompt B:** few-shot. Well-matched. The classification labels (fruit, vegetable) and the output shape are made unambiguous by the two worked examples. The model continues the pattern on `pumpkin`.
- **Prompt C:** zero-shot CoT (the *"let's think step by step"* form). Well-matched in principle. The arithmetic is genuinely multi-step, so giving the model intermediate tokens helps. Note: a modern instruction-tuned model often gets a problem at this level right zero-shot too; CoT is most clearly worth the token cost on harder multi-step problems.
- **Prompt D:** zero-shot with a role prompt. Partly well-matched. The classification task is real (is this phishing?), but the *"25 years of experience"* persona framing is decoration; the model does not actually have access to 25 years of experience. The lever you want here is *clear criteria* (what to check for) or *few-shot examples of phishing-vs-not*, not the persona.
- **Prompt E:** few-shot CoT. Well-matched. The example demonstrates the reasoning shape, not just the answer. The model is biased to produce a similar reasoning chain on the new question. This is the recipe for math-word-problem prompts where you want both the chain and the final number.
- **Prompt F:** zero-shot. Well-matched. Summarization is well-covered by SFT, and the output shape (three bullets) is enforced clearly enough by the instruction.

### Part two: redesign

Pick **two** of the prompts above that were poorly matched or could be improved. Rewrite them. Then write one sentence about *why* the rewrite should produce better output.

**Hints, not answers:**

- Prompt D is the obvious candidate. The lever is criteria, not persona. A few-shot version with three labeled examples (one phishing, two not, with the giveaway features called out) would beat the role prompt.
- Prompt C is an interesting case. If you find yourself adding *"let's think step by step"* on simple arithmetic that the model gets right zero-shot, you are paying tokens for nothing. Try the same problem zero-shot first; reach for CoT when zero-shot is unreliable.

**Sanity check:** the goal of part two is the muscle, not the right answer. Rewriting prompts and naming why your rewrite should help is the loop you want to build. Most prompt-engineering improvements you will ever make in real work look like this: classify the pattern, ask whether it fits, redesign, and verify.

<FlashcardSet>

<Flashcard question="What is a prompt, mechanically?">

The input tokens you control. The model is still doing next-token prediction; the prompt is the prefix that conditions the loop. The post-training (covered in our Lecture 5 lesson) biases the model to produce a response when the input is instruction-shaped, but the underlying machinery has not changed.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Name the three dominant prompting patterns.">

Zero-shot (just ask), few-shot (show examples then ask), chain-of-thought (produce intermediate reasoning tokens before the final answer). Almost every prompt you write is one of these or a combination.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="When is zero-shot the right default?">

When the task is one that SFT training data covered well (translation, summarization, common classification, simple Q&A, format conversion) and the output format is not picky. Cheapest pattern, easiest to write, leverages the instruction-tuning bias directly.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is in-context learning?">

The phenomenon underlying few-shot prompting. The model sees a structured sequence of input-output examples and infers, from the examples alone, what the next output should be. No weights change at inference time; the "learning" happens in the forward pass, conditioned by the examples in the context window.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Where do few-shot returns diminish?">

Most of the benefit shows up by the third or fourth example for typical tasks. Adding twenty rarely outperforms five well-chosen ones. The marginal example does less and less.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What matters more in few-shot: the wording of the instruction or the structure of the examples?">

The structure of the examples. Changing the wording while keeping example shape identical leaves behavior roughly unchanged; changing the example shape while keeping wording often loses the pattern. Format consistency in the examples is the lever.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Why does chain-of-thought help on multi-step reasoning?">

The model applies the same amount of computation per generated token, so producing more tokens before the final answer gives it more total compute on the problem. Each intermediate token also conditions the next, so the model can decompose a hard problem in writing rather than committing to an answer in one shot.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="When does forced CoT hurt?">

On simple lookup, single-step classification, summarization of straightforward text, most translation. CoT adds tokens, can introduce confident-sounding intermediate errors, and rarely improves accuracy on tasks without a real reasoning chain. Reach for CoT when the task actually has a chain to walk.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is the role of a system prompt?">

A separate, conceptually higher-trust input that sets standing instructions for the conversation (role, style, refusals, constraints). The model is biased to follow it, but treats it as guidance rather than law. Useful when the conversation has consistent role or constraint; not a sandbox.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is prompt injection?">

Text inside an input that the application thinks is data (email, webpage, comment, search result) contains instruction-shaped tokens that the model follows as if they were operator instructions. The model has no robust way to distinguish operator instructions from instructions hidden in user-supplied data; at the token level they are both just text conditioning the next-token loop.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Why is prompt injection structural rather than a bug?">

Because the property that makes the model useful (instruction-following over input tokens) is the same property that makes it follow injected instructions. Mitigations like instruction-hierarchy training, channel separation, output filtering, and sandboxing reduce the gap; they do not close it.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is the one-sentence takeaway from this lesson?">

Zero-shot asks the model. Few-shot shows the model. Chain-of-thought walks the model through it.

</Flashcard>

</FlashcardSet>
