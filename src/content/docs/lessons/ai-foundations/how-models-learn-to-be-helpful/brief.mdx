## What you'll learn

This is the first lesson in our adaptation of Stanford CME 295 Lecture 5 (LLM tuning). It also covers the supervised-fine-tuning topic from Lecture 4 (LLM training), since the SFT-to-RLHF arc is one continuous story and it would be odd to split it across two lessons. The earlier lessons in this track covered the transformer architecture (Lecture 1) and how that architecture generates text at runtime (Lecture 3). What they described is a *base model*: the raw output of pretraining, a transformer that has read a lot of text and gotten good at predicting the next token. A base model is not yet a chat assistant. The thing you actually talk to has been through two more training stages after pretraining, and those stages are why it feels helpful instead of just plausible. This lesson walks through both of them: supervised fine-tuning, where the model learns to follow instructions, and reinforcement learning from human feedback, where it learns which kinds of responses people rated higher in training. Course materials are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/).

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the attention lesson (Lecture 1) and the text generation lesson (Lecture 3) are strongly recommended. Attention is the mechanism the assistant builds on; text generation explains what "the model produces tokens one at a time" actually means in operation. We assume both are familiar. If "logits," "softmax," or "next-token prediction" feel unfamiliar, read those lessons first.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Explain the three-stage training pipeline (pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, RLHF) and what each stage adds to a base model
- Describe what supervised fine-tuning does and why instruction-following emerges from it
- Walk through the RLHF loop in plain language (reward model, policy optimization, the human-feedback signal)
- Identify which observable behaviors of modern AI assistants come from pretraining versus fine-tuning versus RLHF

## Time and difficulty

- **Read time:** about 25 minutes
- **Practice time:** about 15 minutes (a comparison exercise: identify which stage produced which observed behavior on real assistant outputs)
