## Going deeper

A short list, chosen for durability. Each link is for a specific next step, not a generic "learn more."

- **["Illustrating Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)"](https://huggingface.co/blog/rlhf)** by Nathan Lambert and colleagues on the Hugging Face blog. The clearest end-to-end visual walk-through of the canonical RLHF pipeline (SFT, reward modeling, PPO) on the public web. Diagrams that map directly to the three-stage model in this lesson. Read this if you want the picture-version of what we covered in prose.

- **["Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)**, Ouyang et al., 2022. The InstructGPT paper. The first widely discussed end-to-end demonstration that SFT followed by RLHF produces a model that humans clearly prefer over the base model on instruction-following tasks. The methodology section is readable and short; the appendix has the actual labeling instructions, which are more useful than they sound for understanding what "preference" actually meant in practice.

- **["Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)**, Rafailov et al., 2023. The DPO paper. Derives the loss function that lets you skip the separate reward model entirely. The math is more accessible than most RL papers because DPO collapses an RL problem into a supervised one. Read after you understand canonical RLHF; the contrast is the point.

- **["Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)**, Bai et al., 2022 (Anthropic). The CAI paper. Walks through using AI-generated critiques (working from a written set of principles) to replace some human comparisons. Useful for understanding the RLAIF family of approaches and the tradeoff at its core: less human labeling cost, more dependence on the principles you wrote down.

- **[Stanford CME 295 cheatsheet](https://cme295.stanford.edu/cheatsheet/)** by the Amidi twins. MIT-licensed. The post-pretraining section gives a one-page reference for the same material this lesson covers, in their dense visual style. Print it; it pairs well with this lesson's flashcards as a single-page review surface.

## Adjacent topics

Topics that build on or sit beside this one.

- **Reward model overoptimization (Goodhart's law in RLHF).** A separate body of work studies how aggressively the policy can optimize against the reward model before the proxy breaks down. The reward model is not the human; the more you push, the more the divergence shows. Critical reading for anyone working on alignment in production.

- **Process supervision and step-level rewards.** Recent work on math and reasoning has shifted toward rewarding intermediate reasoning steps (not just final answers). Trades labeling cost for a much denser training signal. Sometimes called "PRM" (process reward model) versus "ORM" (outcome reward model).

- **RLAIF in practice.** Several open-source instruction-tuned models now use AI-generated preference data as a primary or supplementary signal. The empirical question (how close does AI feedback get to human-quality preferences?) is active research; Constitutional AI is the best-known starting point.

- **Continued pretraining versus SFT.** The lesson's "common pitfalls" section flags the boundary. Continued pretraining injects new domain knowledge into the base model; SFT teaches response shape on knowledge already there. The line is fuzzy in practice; many open-source domain-specific models combine both stages.

- **Where to go next.** This lesson closes the post-training arc of the Stanford POC track. If you want to keep going on AI fundamentals, the natural next stop will be agentic AI and tool use (a planned lesson, not yet published; check the [tracks index](/tracks/) for the latest). If you want to broaden out, Track 1 covers the human side of working with AI; both tracks are independent of each other.

## Original sources

The primary papers for the techniques covered.

- **["Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741)**, Christiano et al., 2017. The paper that introduced the core RLHF method (preference comparisons used to train a reward model used to optimize a policy). Predates language model applications by several years; the original use case was robotic control. Worth skimming for the conceptual root.

- **["Learning to summarize with human feedback"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325)**, Stiennon et al., 2020. The first large-scale application of RLHF to language model outputs (text summarization), and the direct methodological predecessor to InstructGPT. The paper where RLHF on language models started working well enough to matter.

- **["Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)**, Schulman et al., 2017. The PPO paper. The RL algorithm that the "RL" in canonical RLHF refers to. Dense by RL-paper standards but the canonical reference; most RLHF implementations cite this directly.
