## What you'll learn

This is the third lesson in our adaptation of Stanford CME 295 Lecture 2 (Transformer-based models & tricks). The previous two lessons covered position embeddings and normalization, the first two of three places the field genuinely changed after 2017. This lesson covers the third: attention efficiency. Specifically, two distinct problems with two distinct fixes. The first is the **compute problem**: standard self-attention is O(n^2) in sequence length, which becomes a bottleneck at the long contexts modern LLMs run on. The fix is **sliding window attention**: each token attends only to its local neighborhood. The second is the **memory problem**: the KV cache that makes decoding fast also gets large fast. The fix is **sharing key and value projections across attention heads**, in a progression from multi-head attention (MHA) through multi-query attention (MQA) to group-query attention (GQA). The lecture treats these as two orthogonal efficiency moves; this lesson keeps them cleanly separate. Course materials are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/).

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the multi-head attention lesson and the transformer block lesson (both in our Lecture 1 adaptation) are required reading. We assume you understand what an attention head is, what `Q`, `K`, `V` projection matrices are, and what the attention computation looks like. If those terms feel unfamiliar, read the corresponding Lecture 1 lesson first.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Explain why standard self-attention is O(n^2) in compute and where that becomes a real bottleneck
- Describe sliding window attention and the receptive-field analogy that explains how local layers still see distant tokens through stacking
- Distinguish the compute problem (attention complexity) from the memory problem (KV cache size during decoding); explain why they need different fixes
- Walk through the MHA -> MQA -> GQA progression and explain why most modern LLMs land on GQA

## Time and difficulty

- **Read time:** about 22 minutes
- **Practice time:** about 15 minutes (a complexity-counting exercise on a small attention matrix, plus a comparison of KV-cache sizes across MHA, MQA, and GQA configurations)
- **Difficulty:** standard
