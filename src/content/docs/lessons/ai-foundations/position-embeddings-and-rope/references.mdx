## Going deeper

A short list, chosen for durability. Each link is for a specific next step, not a generic "learn more."

- **["Attention Is All You Need"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)**, Vaswani et al., 2017. The original transformer paper. Section 3.5 ("Positional Encoding") is the source for the learned-vs-sinusoidal discussion in this lesson. Read it after this lesson; the formula will be familiar and the trade-offs will already make sense.

- **["RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864)**, Su et al., 2021. The RoPE paper. Section 3 derives the result that the dot product of rotated vectors depends on relative position; the appendix has the long-term-decay upper bound the lecture mentions. Read after the RoPE section in this lesson; the math will be more accessible than it looks because you already know what it is trying to prove.

- **["Train Short, Test Long: Attention with Linear Biases Enables Input Length Extrapolation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12409)**, Press et al., 2021. The ALiBi paper. The empirical demonstration that a deterministic linear bias inside the attention softmax extrapolates better than learned position embeddings or even sinusoidal. Worth reading for the contrast with RoPE (which won the broader race despite ALiBi being earlier).

- **["Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified Text-to-Text Transformer"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)**, Raffel et al., 2020. The T5 paper. The relative-position-bias scheme is described in section 2.1; you can skip the rest unless you want the full T5 context (which is the topic of the Lecture 2.4 lesson, coming later).

- **[Stanford CME 295 cheatsheet](https://cme295.stanford.edu/cheatsheet/)** by the Amidi twins. MIT-licensed. The position-embedding section of the cheatsheet gives a one-page reference for the same material this lesson covers, in their dense visual style.

## Adjacent topics

Topics that build on or sit beside this one.

- **RoPE scaling and long-context extension.** The lecture stops at the basic RoPE formulation. A growing literature studies how to remap the rotation frequencies so a model trained on a moderate sequence length can effectively attend to a much longer context post-training (sometimes called "RoPE scaling," "linear interpolation of positions," "NTK-aware scaling," and other names). Search terms: "RoPE position interpolation," "YaRN," "ABF (adjusted base frequency)." Worth knowing exists; the field is moving quickly.

- **Sinusoidal frequency intuition.** The original sinusoidal scheme uses a frequency spectrum spanning many orders of magnitude (the `10000^(2i/d_model)` in the formula). 3Blue1Brown's [video on Fourier series](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spUNpyF58BY) is the gentlest entry point if you want to understand why a sum of sines and cosines at different frequencies can encode a wide range of patterns.

- **The geometric intuition for high-dimensional rotations.** RoPE rotates query and key block-by-block through 2D pieces of high-dimensional vectors. The intuition for why this composes cleanly comes from the math of orthogonal matrices, of which rotations are a special case. Search terms: "block-diagonal rotation matrix," "SO(2) representation."

- **Where to go next.** The next lesson in this lecture covers **layer normalization** (the second of the three places where modern transformers genuinely diverge from the 2017 paper). After that, **attention efficiency tricks** (sliding window attention and the MHA → MQA → GQA progression). Both build on the Lecture 1 architecture knowledge you already have.
