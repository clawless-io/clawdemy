## What you'll learn

This is the first lesson in our adaptation of Stanford CME 295 Lecture 2 (Transformer-based models & tricks). Lecture 1 covered the original transformer paper from 2017. Lecture 2 is about the parts of that architecture that have evolved since then, and which evolutions have actually stuck. The first such part is **position embeddings**: how the model knows that "the cat sat on the mat" is different from "the mat sat on the cat" when the attention mechanism on its own treats the input as a bag of tokens. The original transformer's answer was sinusoidal embeddings added to the input; modern LLMs almost all use a different scheme called **RoPE** (rotary position embeddings). This lesson walks the path from one to the other and explains why the field landed where it did. The full course materials (syllabus, schedule, the Amidi cheatsheets) are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/).

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the attention lesson and the transformer block lesson (both in our Lecture 1 adaptation) are required reading. We assume you understand what a query, key, and value vector are, what the dot product `Q · K^T` represents in self-attention, and what it means to "add" a vector to the input embedding. If any of those terms feel unfamiliar, read the corresponding Lecture 1 lesson first.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Explain why self-attention requires an explicit position signal and what would go wrong without one
- Describe the original transformer paper's two position-embedding options (learned and sinusoidal) and the trade-off between them
- Identify the structural shift between input-added position embeddings and attention-injected position embeddings, and name the two early attention-injected variants (T5 relative bias, ALiBi)
- Walk through what RoPE (rotary position embeddings) actually does in plain language, and explain why most modern LLMs use it

## Time and difficulty

- **Read time:** about 25 minutes
- **Practice time:** about 15 minutes (a worked sinusoidal-embedding computation, plus a paired-comparison exercise on which scheme would do what on a given input)
- **Difficulty:** standard
