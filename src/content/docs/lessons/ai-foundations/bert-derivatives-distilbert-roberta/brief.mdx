## What you'll learn

This is lesson 9 of Phase 2, *How models think: the transformer architecture*, in Track 5 (AI Foundations). This is the Phase 2 closer. The previous lesson covered BERT itself: the encoder-only architecture, structural tokens (CLS, SEP), MLM and NSP pretraining, and the train-then-fine-tune workflow. This lesson covers two of the most influential **derivatives** that built on BERT to address its limitations. **DistilBERT** addressed the latency/size problem through **knowledge distillation**: train a smaller "student" model to mimic the output distribution of a larger "teacher" model. **RoBERTa** addressed the pretraining-complexity problem by dropping the NSP objective entirely (it turned out not to help) and showing that just training MLM longer on more data hurts nothing. Both are short papers with outsized influence. Course materials are at [cme295.stanford.edu](https://cme295.stanford.edu/).

## Before you start

**Prerequisites:** the [BERT lesson](/lessons/ai-foundations/bert-architecture-and-objectives/lesson/) is required. We assume you understand what BERT's MLM and NSP pretraining objectives do, what fine-tuning means, and what a 100-million-parameter encoder feels like in terms of compute. If those feel unfamiliar, read the BERT lesson first.

## By the end, you'll be able to

- Identify the three limitations of the original BERT model the lecturer flags (context length, latency/size, pretraining complexity)
- Explain knowledge distillation as a concept (teacher/student, soft targets, KL divergence) and why it produces a smaller-but-comparable student model
- Describe what DistilBERT does specifically (halve the layer count, train via distillation against the original BERT) and the empirical result
- Walk through RoBERTa's three changes (drop NSP, dynamic masking, much more data) and what each one revealed about the original BERT recipe
