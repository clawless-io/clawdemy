## Self-check

Six short questions. Answer each in your head before opening the collapsible. Active retrieval is where the learning sticks.

**1. The framework splits its work into twelve agents. What principle decides the split?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Function. The agents are grouped into clusters that each do a distinct kind of work: analysts gather information, researchers argue, a trader synthesizes, risk seats stress-test, and managers judge. The team is one workflow decomposed along its natural seams; the headcount falls out of that, it is not the goal.

</details>

**2. Name the five functional clusters and what each does.**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Analysts (gather and read raw information), researchers (argue opposite sides, a bull and a bear), trader (turn the argument into a concrete plan), risk seats (stress-test the plan from three temperaments), and managers (judge: one rules the debate, one makes the final call).

</details>

**3. The file imports the agents in one order, but they do not run in that order. What is the runtime pipeline?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Analysts gather, then the bull and bear argue, then the research manager rules on the argument, then the trader turns the ruling into a plan, then the three risk seats stress-test it, then the portfolio manager makes the final call. The two judges sit at two different points in the flow, not side by side.

</details>

**4. The system runs on two model tiers. Which agents get the more capable "deep" model, and why those?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Exactly two: the research manager and the portfolio manager, the two judges. They are the points where one agent weighs everyone else's work and commits, so a wrong call there is the most expensive. Capability is a budget; you spend it where decisions are made.

</details>

**5. A teammate says "so the lesson is that you need twelve agents." What is the more precise takeaway?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

The number is not the design. Twelve is just the count when all four (selectable) analysts are on. The real, invariant design is: decompose the workflow into roles by function, and give your most capable model only to the small number of judgment nodes. Change the analyst count and the headcount shifts, but the deep model still goes to exactly the two judges.

</details>

**6. What is a "factory" here, and why does it matter for building your own team?**

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

Each `create_*` is a factory: call it and it returns one configured agent node, with the model it should use passed in as an argument. That single design choice is what makes a role replaceable: you can improve or swap one agent without touching the wiring or the other agents. It is the seam you build against.

</details>

## Try it yourself: decompose a workflow and place the judges

No tooling, no cost; this is design judgment. Pick a task you understand well that is not stock analysis (for example: triaging incoming support tickets, or producing a vetted research brief). Then:

```
1. List the distinct FUNCTIONS the work contains (gather, analyze, argue,
   synthesize, check, decide). Each becomes a candidate role.
2. For each role, write its one-line job and the few tools it would need.
3. Circle the JUDGMENT NODES: the points where one agent commits on behalf of
   the rest. Those get your most capable model; the rest run on a cheaper one.
```

<details>
<summary>Show answer (worked example: support-ticket triage)</summary>

- **Functions / roles:** an intake reader (summarize the ticket), a history gatherer (pull this customer's prior tickets, a tool-using role), a severity classifier, a routing proposer, and a **triage judge** that commits the final priority and queue.
- **Tools:** the history gatherer needs ticket-search; the others mostly reason over what is in shared state.
- **Judgment node:** the triage judge is the one place a wrong call is expensive (a mis-routed P1). Give it your strongest model; the readers and classifiers can run on a cheaper, faster one.

Notice the shape matches the lesson: many cheap gatherers and analyzers, one or two expensive deciders. The domain changed; the pattern did not.

</details>

<Flashcard question="What are the five functional clusters in this framework?">

<Flashcard question="Why a separate bull agent and bear agent instead of one balanced agent?">

A single agent asked to weigh pros and cons tends to talk itself into a tidy, balanced answer. Two adversaries each argue one side at full strength, so both cases get made properly.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Is import order the same as run order?">

No. At run time: analysts, then bull and bear debate, then research manager rules, then trader plans, then three risk seats, then portfolio manager decides. The two judges sit at different points in the flow.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="Which agents get the deep (more capable) model, and how many?">

Exactly two: the research manager and the portfolio manager, the two judges. The other ten agents run on the cheaper, faster quick model.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is the invariant that matters more than the number twelve?">

The deep model always goes to exactly the two judges, no matter how many analysts you run. Decompose into roles by function, then spend capability only at the judgment nodes.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What is a 'factory' in this codebase?">

A create_* function that returns one configured agent node, with its model injected as an argument. It is what makes a role replaceable without touching the wiring.

</Flashcard>

<Flashcard question="What are the first two steps to design your own agent team?">

1. Decompose the workflow into functions, each a role with narrow instructions and few tools. 2. Find the judgment nodes (where one agent decides for the rest) and put your best model only there.

</Flashcard>
